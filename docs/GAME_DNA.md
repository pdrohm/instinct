# Game DNA

> The immutable identity of the game. This is philosophy, not implementation.
> If a future decision contradicts this document, the decision is wrong — or this document must be changed deliberately, in writing, by the person who owns the vision. It should almost never change.
> Companion to `VISION.md`. Governed alongside `DESIGN_PRINCIPLES.md`, `PLAYER_EXPERIENCE.md`, `DESIGN_DECISIONS.md`, `DESIGN_QUESTIONS.md`.

## Core identity

This is a game about **being another living thing in a world that does not care whether you live.**

Not commanding it. Not optimizing it. *Being* it — inside its body, its senses, its instincts, and its limits — inside a prehistoric ecosystem that runs on its own logic whether or not you are watching.

The ecosystem is the product. Species are perspectives into it. Everything else is detail.

## Core fantasy

*"For a while, I stopped being a person and became an animal — and I survived on its terms, not mine."*

The player trades human tools (menus, objectives, guarantees, a world built for them) for animal ones (senses, instinct, body, and the hard arithmetic of energy and risk). The reward is a kind of experience games rarely offer: not power, but **presence** — the feeling of genuinely inhabiting a life not your own.

## What makes this game unique

- **You are one life among many, not the center.** Most games make the world revolve around the player. Here the world would carry on identically if you vanished. That indifference is not a flaw to fix — it is the entire emotional foundation.
- **The same world is a different game through each species.** One simulation, many bodies. The wolf, the horse, the mammoth, the human each meet the *same* terrain, herd, and winter as an entirely different problem, because their instincts and bodies differ. No one else is building "one ecosystem, many lenses."
- **Stories come from systems, not scripts.** The memorable moments are generated by simple survival rules colliding, never authored by a designer. This is the moat and the risk in one sentence.
- **Prehistoric survival with real teeth, grounded in real biology** — used as inspiration for mechanics, not as decoration.

## What emotions the game should create

In rough order of importance:

1. **Presence** — the sense of actually being an animal, not driving one.
2. **Tension** — the honest weight of a decision that can cost you.
3. **Vulnerability** — the awareness that the world is larger and more dangerous than you.
4. **Relief** — the exhale after barely surviving.
5. **Belonging / loss** — the pull of herd, pack, family, and the ache when it breaks.
6. **Wonder** — quiet awe at a living world going about its business.

Notably **absent**: mastery-as-domination, power fantasy, completion satisfaction. Those emotions are not what this game is for. If a feature's main payoff is one of them, it is probably fighting the DNA.

## What players should remember years later

Not a boss, a build, or a number. A **moment that felt like theirs** — a decision, a near-death, a loss, a survival — that the game did not hand them but that emerged from their own choices inside an indifferent world. The proof of success is that two players' strongest memories are completely different from each other.

## What the game is NOT

- **Not a hunting game.** Hunting is one species' expression of a universal survival loop. Framing the whole game around it is a category error.
- **Not a power fantasy.** The player does not become stronger than the world. Growth is understanding, not dominance.
- **Not a combat game.** Conflict exists; combat-as-primary-verb does not. (See `DESIGN_QUESTIONS.md` — resisting the gravity toward combat is a permanent, active discipline.)
- **Not a survival-crafting / base-building game.** No inventories-as-gameplay, no tech trees, no fortresses.
- **Not a theme park built around the player.** No quests, no objective markers as the spine, no world that pauses or bends for you.
- **Not a realism simulator.** Real animal life is often idle, repetitive, and brutal. We take biology as inspiration, not as a mandate. (See the tension in `DESIGN_DECISIONS.md`.)
- **Not a graphics showcase.** Believable behavior beats photoreal fidelity, always.

## What should never happen

- The world should never feel like it exists *for* the player.
- The player should never stop feeling like the animal — no ability, UI, or convenience should shatter the sense of inhabiting a body.
- A species should never collapse into "the combat species." Each must survive through its own nature.
- A survival system should never become busywork — a bar to top off with no real decision behind it.
- Emergence should never be faked with scripted "random events." If it did not come from the simulation, it does not belong.
- Complexity should never be added for the appearance of depth.

## What should always happen

- Every meaningful action should carry a real cost and a real risk.
- The world should keep living whether the player acts or not.
- The player should be able to *feel* their animal's state — hunger, exhaustion, fear — through experience, not just read it off a UI.
- Every species should experience the same world differently.
- Small systems should be preferred over big features, so emergence has room to happen.
- When we bend biology for fun, we should know we are doing it and why.

## How success should be measured

Success is **not** playtime, retention curves, or feature count. Those are business metrics, and we will track them, but they are not how we know the *game* is working.

The game is working when:

1. **Naive players tell unscripted survival stories** — specific, personal, different from each other's.
2. **Players describe the experience as *being* the animal**, not controlling it.
3. **Watching the world is compelling even when the player does nothing.**
4. **Two different species feel like two different games** to the same player.

If those four are true, the DNA is intact regardless of what the dashboards say. If they are false, no amount of content fixes it.

## Experiences we want players to tell each other

These are illustrations of the *shape* of a good story — not features to build:

- *"I watched a herd for five minutes before I moved. Then I picked the wrong one to chase and went hungry."*
- *"I was so focused on the hunt I didn't notice the bigger predator until it was too late. I barely made it out."*
- *"I found a spot that felt safe, and for the first time all night I could rest."*
- *"I lost track of my herd in the storm and spent the whole winter alone."*
- *"Nothing dramatic happened. I just survived another day. And somehow that stuck with me."*

The last one matters most. If the *quiet* survival is memorable, the DNA is doing its job.

## The one-sentence test

Before shipping anything, ask: **"Does this make the player feel more like a living thing in an indifferent world — or more like a player using a game?"**

If it is the second, it is probably wrong, no matter how good it is in isolation.
